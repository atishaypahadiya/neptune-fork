## Claims

1. The volume of manuscripts, given by Mrs Adams to St John's College, "contains all, or very
   nearly all" that Adams wrote on the perturbations of Uranus, from the memorandum of 1841 to
   the discovery; Sampson ordered it and wrote its catalogue. [read: p. 143]
2. Adams's observations: to 1821, Bouvard's residuals of his equations of condition; from 1821
   on, collected from *Astronomische Nachrichten* and elsewhere, and from Cambridge and
   Greenwich. [read: pp. 144, 154]
3. In the 1843 solution Adams "makes a mistake, forgetting to divide by a factor 3", corrected
   on the manuscript; that solution gives conjunction closely but "the position indicated in
   1843 was some 15° in error". [read: p. 145]
4. "From first to last Adams made no less than six separate solutions": I 1843 (C.II); II
   undated (E.I); III dated 1845 April 28 (E.II); IV dated 1845 September 18, "the solution
   communicated to Challis", containing "a trifling error"; V the first hypothesis of the
   memoir, communicated to Airy in 1845; VI the second hypothesis (a/a′ = 0·515, a′ = 37·25),
   "completed at the end of August 1846". [read: pp. 145–146]
5. Of IV: "At any date between 1813 and 1866 it marks a position of the planet within 5° of
   the truth." [read: p. 146]
6. The attempts on the node and inclination from the latitudes "must be called unsuccessful";
   the apse and eccentricity "represent nothing except the effort of the mechanical process of
   solution to compensate the excessive value attributed to the mean distance". [read: p. 146]
7. The true perturbations by Neptune are "of a character almost totally different" from those
   Adams and Le Verrier assumed: most is absorbed in a long inequality "of period about 4220
   years", and some principal terms have the opposite sign. [read: pp. 147–149; the tables of
   coefficients not relied on]
8. Peirce's objection (commensurable orbits between the supposed and the true one) is granted
   "a basis of truth": the term in 2(n − n′) changes sign through n = 2n′. [read: p. 149]
9. On shorter methods: "It has often been suggested that a much less elaborate discussion …
   would have indicated a position of the planet that was practically correct. I believe that
   this is a delusion"; Herschel's graphical argument for conjunction about 1822 is "entirely
   baseless" though its conclusion was true. [read: p. 149]
10. A table sets Le Verrier's unexplained errors, 1781–1845, beside the actual perturbations
    from Newcomb's tables, split into short and long period; "no such resemblance can be
    traced". [read: p. 150; the table's digits not relied on]
11. The table of six determinations at epoch 1810·328 and the comparison with Neptune's
    longitude, 1800–1850. [read: pp. 151–152 as OCR, garbled; the values are in
    `P1B_adams_solutions.md`, from the page image]
12. The dates at which each solution's place is correct are I 1820, II 1835, III 1872, IV 1830,
    V 1861, VI 1856. [read: p. 152, OCR; this line is clean]
13. The eccentricity helped "not because the eccentricity and perihelion as determined were
    correct, but because their introduction permitted the equations to rectify to some degree
    the excessive value assumed for the mean distance, by placing perihelion in the region of
    the planet's motion which was under review". [read: p. 152]
14. The better agreement after 1810 than before "appears to be an accidental circumstance. It
    is one of the many dramatic but irrelevant features which enhanced the interest of the
    discovery". [read: p. 153]
15. "The prediction of Neptune's place was a more delicate and a somewhat more precarious
    matter than has sometimes been supposed." [read: p. 154]
16. The catalogue's sections: A collection of observations; B corrections to the tables of
    Uranus; C the solution of 1843; D formation of equations for the final solution; E various
    solutions; F errors of the radius vector; G perturbations in latitude; H rough
    calculations. [read: headings only, pp. 154–170]

## What it means for this repository

Sampson is used in the repository as the catalogue of Adams's solutions and that use stands.
What this reading adds is that his essay already makes three of the companion study's points in
words, without a noise study: the eccentricity and apse compensate the over-large distance
(claims 6 and 13), the prediction was "precarious" and its timing partly "accidental" (14, 15),
and the intervals of validity of each solution are tabulated (5, 12). The reproduction's finding
that the shortfall at a held 38.4 AU belongs to the assumed distance is therefore a
quantification of something Sampson states qualitatively. Claim 9 is in tension with Lyttleton
1958, who built exactly such a shorter method half a century later; Lyttleton does not cite
Sampson in the pages I read. Claim 2 is the documentary basis for asking what Bouvard's tables
carried into Adams's data (`P1B_bouvard_attribution.md`); Sampson does not, in pp. 143–154,
attempt that attribution.
