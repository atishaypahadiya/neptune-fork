## Claims

1. The paper's thesis: unpublished documents, read after the Greenwich "Neptune file"
   reappeared in 1999, fail "to endorse the status traditionally assigned to John Couch Adams
   … as co-predictor of Neptune's position". [read: Part 1 p. 1]
2. "The sky-sweep began at Cambridge on 29 July 1846 … and it lasted two months." [read: Part 1
   p. 18, as quoted in `P1B_sources.md`]
3. Adams's second hypothesis, "although worked through to a higher level of accuracy, was less
   accurate, the first being within two degrees and the second, three (Appendix II)". [read:
   Part 1 p. 19]
4. On 2 September 1846 Adams wrote that the mean longitude "would be 315° 20′, adding 'which I
   am inclined to think is not far from the truth'". [read: Part 1 p. 20, one line]
5. Note 126: Brookes 1970 "esp. p. 71: 'the first hypothesis was the more accurate of the
   two'"; Smart "(ref. 16, p. 55) argued au contraire that Adams's second hypothesis had 'a
   small advantage over the first'". Note 128: a "discontinuity at 35.3 AU" near the 5:2
   resonance, "as was argued by Benjamin Peirce", and Brookes p. 78 with "317° at 34 AU".
   [read: Part 1 p. 28]
6. Galle found the planet with the Berlin map; Le Verrier's prediction was 52 arcminutes away
   (Fig. 5 caption); "Neptune was found at 327°, Leverrier's prediction was for 326°, and
   Adams's alleged predictions were around 329° helio-longitude". [read: Part 2 pp. 349–350]
7. Challis "had seen Neptune twice, on 4 and 12 August 1846"; on 17 October he gave an account
   of the search in *The Athenaeum*. [read: Part 2 p. 352]
8. Kollerstrom endorses Rawlins's thesis "that Adams had no confidence in any solutions he
   reached, prior to 2 September 1846". [read: Part 2 p. 357]
9. "In April, using the Lalande 'missing star' position, Adams was prepared to express the
   eccentricity of the orbit as 0.0093"; the April is that of 1847 by the context (the sentence
   follows the orbit printed on 12 March). [read: Part 2 p. 361]
10. The 2:1 near-resonance "had been wholly unsuspected by Adams or Le Verrier"; Peirce called
    the discovery a "happy accident"; Walker: "the present visible planet Neptune is not the
    mathematical planet to which theory had directed the telescope". [read: Part 2 p. 361]
11. Appendix I, distances in AU: Le Verrier final 36.2 (perihelion 32.3, at discovery 33.1,
    e 0.107); Adams Hyp. 1 38.4 (32.2, 32.3, 0.161); Adams Hyp. 2 37.5 (33.0, 33.4, 0.120).
    "Adams gave his values as fractions of an unstated Uranus mean-radius, so reconstructions
    vary slightly." [read: Part 2 p. 365, OCR; these agree with `P1B_sources.md`]
12. Appendix II, true heliocentric longitude at 23 September 1846: Adams 1 328° 41′ (error
    1° 44′), Adams 2 329° 27′ (2° 30′), Le Verrier 1 324° 35′ (−2° 21′), Le Verrier 2 325° 58′
    (−0° 58′); Neptune 326° 57′; "identical with those of Rawlins". [read: Part 2 p. 365, OCR;
    agree with `P1B_sources.md`]
13. Appendix III and Fig. 6: the residual curve of Uranus "made no sense to any historian or
    astronomer until the 1990 paper by … Lai, Lam and Young"; the modern perturbation terms
    are "an order of magnitude larger" than the historical ones; the principal term "has a
    maximum of 52 arcminutes over 4,200 years"; the perturbation reached "a minimum over this
    historical period and not a maximum as had been supposed". The lower panel of Fig. 6 is
    reprinted from Lai et al., p. 950. [read: Part 2 pp. 366–367]
14. The memorandum-book entry reads "last one seemed to have a disc", part scratched out.
    [read: Part 1 p. 7, one line; the context was not read]

## Disagreements with the repository's notes

- `P1B_sources.md` § 5 lists Appendices I and II under "Part 1 gives". In the ADS scans they
  are in Part 2, pp. 365–367; Part 1 only refers forward to them (pp. 10, 11, 19). The figures
  themselves agree.
- `P1B_sources.md` cites "note 128" for Brookes p. 78; in the ADS scan the note that names
  Brookes is 126, and 128 is the resonance note that cites him again. No conflict, two notes.

## What it means for this repository

Kollerstrom is a documentary history; in what I read it computes nothing beyond converting the
published elements to the discovery date (Appendix II, by his account matching Rawlins) and
taking means and standard deviations of Adams's two residual lists (Part 1 p. 19, the table
itself not read). So the reproduction of Adams's solutions from the memoir and the noise study
are not anticipated in the pages read. His reading that Adams lacked confidence before
2 September 1846 (claim 8) is an argument from documents, and the companion study's question
("luck or not") is a different one; the two should not be presented as the same finding. Claim
9 matters for the Lalande check in `docs/notes/M6e.md`: Lalande's 1795 place was used
historically, after the discovery, to fix the orbit; it was not, in what I read, set against a
pre-discovery prediction. I did not read the sections on Challis's search beyond claims 2, 7
and 14, so nothing here says whether Kollerstrom estimates a chance of detection; in the pages
read he does not.
